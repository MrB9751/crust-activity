Reading: Characteristics of the Crust

Earth's outermost layer is the crust: a cold, thin, brittle shell of solid rock. Compared with the whole planet, the crust is remarkably thin. Earth's radius is about 6,371 kilometers, but the crust is only a few kilometers to a few tens of kilometers thick. If Earth were an apple, the crust would be thinner than its skin.

Picture the surface of a loaf of bread that has risen unevenly. Some parts bulge upward and other parts sag. Earth's crust is similar: the high-standing parts are the continents, and the low-lying parts are the ocean basins. These two regions are made of two very different kinds of crust — oceanic crust and continental crust.

Oceanic Crust

Oceanic crust is relatively thin, usually between about 5 and 12 kilometers thick. It is also dense, averaging about 3.0 grams per cubic centimeter (g/cm³).

Oceanic crust is mafic, meaning its minerals are rich in magnesium and iron. The upper part is made mostly of basalt, a dark, fine-grained igneous rock. Beneath the basalt lies gabbro, which has the same composition as basalt but larger crystals because it cooled more slowly underground.

Resting on top of the oceanic crust is a layer of seafloor sediments, made mostly of mud and the broken shells of tiny sea creatures. This sediment layer is thin in the middle of the ocean and becomes thickest near the shore, where rivers and coastlines deliver large amounts of material from the land.

Continental Crust

Continental crust is much thicker than oceanic crust — about 35 kilometers thick on average, and thicker still beneath large mountain ranges. It is also less dense, averaging about 2.7 g/cm³.

Continental crust is felsic, meaning it is rich in lighter-colored minerals such as feldspar and quartz (silica). Overall, its average composition is similar to granite. However, continental crust is far more varied than oceanic crust: it contains many different igneous, metamorphic, and sedimentary rocks.

Why Continents Stand High

The crust "floats" on the denser mantle beneath it, somewhat like blocks of wood floating in water. A thick block of light wood rides higher than a thin block of heavy wood. In the same way, continental crust — which is both thicker and less dense — rises higher above the mantle. Oceanic crust, which is thinner and denser, sits lower. These low areas form basins, and the basins fill with water to become the oceans.
